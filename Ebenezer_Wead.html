Ebenezer Wead (1748-1830): Frontier Settler and Ohio Pioneer

Primary record Local history Family archive Under review
Research note: This page distinguishes Ebenezer's own still-unlocated Ohio farm from the later Oakwood Wead-family tract associated in local records with John and Robert Wead and their heirs. A family association does not, by itself, prove a chain of title. It also treats the Lee's Legion / bounty-land identity of Ebenezer Wood as an active working hypothesis rather than a settled alias for Ebenezer Wead.

Origins in the Pennsylvania frontier

Ebenezer Wead - also recorded as Wade and Waid - was born in 1748 in the Cumberland Valley of Pennsylvania. His father, John Wead, had established the family near Rocky Spring in what became Franklin County. A separate Revolutionary War record set names an Ebenezer Wood; whether that soldier is the same person as Ebenezer Wead remains an active identity question rather than a settled alias.

John prepared a will in 1760 during a dangerous period on the frontier, but that document should not be treated as proof that he died then. He survived for decades, executed another will in 1789, and died in 1790. The 1760 document is nevertheless important because it directed that the family land pass to young Ebenezer and referred to money due John for provincial military service.

The 1765 Pennsylvania survey

A proprietary survey made on 28 August 1765, pursuant to a warrant dated 24 May 1765, recorded 179 acres and 25 perches for Ebenezer. The survey referred to an earlier improvement made by his father. The difference between an earlier 200-acre warrant description and the returned survey is not necessarily a contradiction: warrants authorized an approximate quantity, while surveys measured the land on the ground.

Tax records and the Ebenezer Wood identity question

Ebenezer appears in a 1783 York County tax list as "Ebenezar Wead," head of a household containing eleven inhabitants. Other records place him in the wider Cumberland-Franklin County region before his westward migration.

The family archive also contains affidavits, pension-related records, and bounty-land materials that may connect the soldier recorded as Ebenezer Wood in Lee's Legion with Ebenezer Wead. Because the current evidence is still being tested, this page treats the connection as a working identity hypothesis. Before it is presented as a firm conclusion, the chain should reconcile age, residence, family witnesses, heirs, land transactions, and any negative evidence pointing to a different Ebenezer Wood.

Migration to the Dayton frontier

Family and local-history evidence places Ebenezer's household in Kentucky during the late 1790s and in the Dayton-area frontier by about 1799. He later appears in early Montgomery County records and is associated with Van Buren Township. He died in 1830 and is buried in Beavertown Cemetery.

Two different land questions

1. Ebenezer's personal Ohio farm

The exact parcel owned or occupied by Ebenezer himself has not yet been established. Locating it requires his Montgomery County deed chain, tax duplicates, probate land description, road records, and neighboring owners. Until those records close the chain, the map uses only a broad Van Buren Township research zone.

2. The later Oakwood Wead-family tract

A separate and much better documented tract in what became Oakwood is associated in local-history sources with John Wead, Robert Wead, and later Wead heirs. Oakwood historical research describes Patterson-family acreage passing to John and Robert, later through John's daughter Nancy Davis and her heirs, and finally back into John H. Patterson's control in the early twentieth century.

This tract is clearly part of the wider Wead family's Montgomery County history, but it should not be labeled "Ebenezer's farm" unless deeds or probate records prove that direct connection. David Alan Schmidt also cautioned that he could not confirm whether the Robert associated with the Oakwood acreage was Ebenezer's son Robert E. Wead (1781-1857).

Wead Road, Wade Road, and Patterson Road

The road evidence now forms a useful sequence. An 1872 Montgomery County road record shows land marked J. S. Wade along the James J. Bradford Road proceeding. A 1901 county survey explicitly labels Wade Road at the Dayton and Lebanon Pike, with Patterson ownership beside the junction. Oakwood historical research states that John H. Patterson successfully petitioned in 1909 to rename historic Wead Road as Patterson Road.

Two other names must remain separate: Dorothy Lane was formerly O'Neil Road, while John H. Patterson Boulevard appears in another proposed or early naming context. Neither is the replacement name for Wead Road.

The exact Library of Congress "Wade tract" file

The remembered archive item is now identified as Olmsted Associates Job 6720, cataloged as Patterson, John H.; Wade tract, subdivision "Woodside"; Dayton, Ohio, 1919-1921. The job file contains correspondence, field notes, road profiles, topographic studies, and a General Plan for Woodside.

The file's "Wade" spelling is consistent with the family's long documentary spelling drift. Contemporary 1917-1918 advertisements for the neighboring McCann development call the same historical setting a subdivision of the Wead Farm. The map project therefore preserves each source's wording while normalizing the identity as the Wead-family tract, archival spelling "Wade."

Repeated control features - Patterson Road, Shafor Boulevard, Schantz Avenue, Irving Avenue, and the Cincinnati, Lebanon & Northern Railroad - make the Olmsted plans suitable for georeferencing. The final historic tract polygon will still require reconciliation with deeds and recorded plats.

A separate Babbitt street

Published neighborhood history identifies another Wead Street in the former Babbitt settlement, later called Lawnview Avenue. It lies north of the Oakwood tract and is not the same road as the Oakwood section-line road that became Patterson Road. The original Babbitt plat or Dayton street-renaming ordinance remains the desired primary citation.
