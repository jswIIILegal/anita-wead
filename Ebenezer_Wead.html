Ebenezer Wead (1748–1830): Frontier Patriot and Pioneer

Origins in Letterkenny Township, Pennsylvania

Ebenezer Wead (also recorded as Wade, Waid, and Wood) was born in 1748 in Letterkenny Township, a frontier region then part of Cumberland County, later Franklin County, Pennsylvania. His father, John Wead, was an early settler who held land documented in Book A, Page 191 of the Pennsylvania land records. This original survey, returned in 1765, covered 179 acres and 25 perches (spelled out in the record; decimal: 179.15625 acres) and referenced prior settlement improvements, likely cabins or clearings made before 1760.

John Wead’s 1760 will was probated shortly after, marking him presumed dead or gravely incapacitated. At just 17 years old, Ebenezer traveled to Philadelphia alone in 1765 to assert his family’s claim—an early display of independence and resilience.

1765 Land Warrant

On May 24, 1765, a proprietary survey recorded 179 acres and 25 perches for Ebenezer Wead in Letterkenny Township, referencing a warrant originally dated August 28, 1765. The warrant notes “an improvement” made by John Wead prior to the survey. This suggests that the land was being reclaimed post-mortem or in trust for the family line.

Key Citation: “A Draught of a Tract of Land situate in Letterkenny Township... surveyed to Ebenezer Wead the 28th Day of August 1765. To pursue a warrant dated May 24th 1765.” — Book A, p.191, Department of Internal Affairs
Acreage notes (200 vs 179 acres + 25 perches)

It’s normal to see both 200 acres and 179 acres + 25 perches in the records. In brief:

  • John Wead (1738, Lancaster County): Period documents plainly state “Two hundred acres” for his warrant. Early Pennsylvania warrants commonly issued for 200 acres.
  • Ebenezer Wead (1765, Letterkenny/Cumberland → Franklin): The returned proprietary survey records 179 acres and 25 perches (Book A, p.191). Pennsylvania surveys often express acreage plus perches (1 acre = 160 perches).
Source Recorded acreage Converted (decimal)
John Wead warrant (ca. 1738, Lancaster) Two hundred acres 200.00 acres
Ebenezer Wead survey (1765, Book A p.191) 179 acres + 25 perches 179 + 25/160 = 179.15625 acres

Warrant vs. survey: a warrant was an authorization (often for 200 acres); a later survey measured the tract(s) on the ground and may differ due to perches, allowances (e.g., for roads), or how parcels were combined/resurveyed. Both figures are accurate in their respective contexts.

Taxation and Residency

Ebenezer appears on the 1783 tax list for York County, Pennsylvania, as “Wead, Ebenezar,” head of a household with 11 inhabitants. He is also found in various Franklin County and Mifflin County tax rolls throughout the 1780s.

These records document his persistent presence across multiple counties prior to his westward migration.

Revolutionary War Service (Alias: Ebenezer Wood)

During the American Revolution, Ebenezer served under the alias Ebenezer Wood in Lee’s Legion, a cavalry unit under “Light Horse Harry” Lee. He was later awarded a bounty land warrant (No. 13926) for his service.

Multiple affidavits and depositions—some decades later—corroborate that the soldier known as Wood was in fact Ebenezer Wead. Supporting documentation includes:

  • Pension applications
  • Affidavits from fellow veterans
  • Bounty land confirmation
“Proving ‘Ebenezer Wood’ (Lee’s Legion) was Ebenezer Wead” — Ref. Military Service Files

These materials, collated in Rev War Bounty Land Warrant #13926, establish a strong evidentiary chain linking his alias to service and identity.

Migration to Ohio and the Dayton Frontier

Around 1799, Ebenezer migrated to Van Buren Township, Montgomery County, Ohio. By 1804, he was documented in the first court records of the region. He appears in Pioneer Life in Dayton and Vicinity, confirming his presence among Dayton’s earliest settlers.

Ebenezer owned multiple tracts of land, including those shown on early 19th-century maps. His descendants remained prominent in the area well into the 1800s.

Wead Road and Legacy

One enduring landmark is Wead Road, named after Ebenezer and preserved on early maps of Colonel Robert Patterson’s holdings in Dayton.

This map was shared by David Schmidt of Wright College and appears in Charlotte Reeve Conover’s Concerning the Forefathers. It confirms Ebenezer’s presence and property footprint adjacent to historic Dayton boundaries.

Burial and Descendants

Ebenezer Wead died in 1830 and was interred in Beavertown Cemetery, Kettering, Ohio. His descendants remained active across Montgomery County, appearing in land maps, tax rolls, and civic records.
